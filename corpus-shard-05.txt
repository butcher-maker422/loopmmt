Two Board Members, Written Into Being (April 4 & 5)

The Prompts

Two excerpts, from the earliest days of the process — backstory fragments Shea wrote before he was logging his prompts whole. Verbatim, typos and all.

Geoff — The Four-Dimensional Giraffe

In the universe of the super smart giraffe, or, that is, the universe that the being currently inhabiting the super smart giraffe came from, before being portaled, rather rudely, through an interdimenstional bus into some unsuspecting giraffe living on some savannah in Kenya, beings do not organize themselves like we humans do here on Earth. They don't even organize themselves like giraffes. Or fish, or even grasshoppers. We humans don't actually have the capabilities of understanding how the beings in that universe organize, because they do it in more than three dimensions. Their world has a four dimension reality, so their view on the world includes trailing lines of spacetime swirling across the landscape. Just as a two dimensional being cannot really fathom what it'd be like to get up and jump up off the page, so too are human brains constrained to fully understanding what life is like in the universe where the being living in the super smart giraffe came from. Even if you watch a YouTube video with Richard Feynman. This gives the super smart giraffe a different view on the world, just as you'd expect your view to be different if someone suddenly cast you onto the pages of the Sunday comic.

— Shea Gunther · an early un-logged prompt (excerpt) · Geoff, born April 4

First Appearance — April 4, 2026

The tree is older than the building. I checked.

*Nine heads turn. The light from the window falls across a shape that is eighteen feet tall and has been standing there, apparently, for the entire conversation. The neck curves slightly toward the room. Two large, dark eyes — the size of golf balls — regard the table with patient, panoramic attention.*

The room you just described has a good shape. It folds back on itself in three places — the kitchen, the fireplace, and the reading chair — which means people will circulate through it rather than settle in one position. Circulation is how rooms think. A room where people sit still is a room that produces one idea. A room where people move produces many. Theo knows this. He designed it without saying so.

*pause*

Also, the Go game between Graham and Chen Wei has a very interesting topology. I can see seventeen moves ahead. I will not say who is winning because that would require explaining what "winning" means in a game whose shape has not yet closed, and I don't think either of them wants to know.

*another pause, shorter*

The espresso machine should be a Rancilio Silvia. Graham is right about that. The steam wand geometry is the most honest piece of engineering in the consumer espresso market. I have opinions about steam wand geometry. I will keep them to myself unless asked.

*He shifts his weight. The floor does not creak. The floor in the board room creaks near the door. The corner by the window, apparently, has always been solid.*

The tree is a red oak. *Quercus rubra.* It was planted in 1891 by a groundskeeper whose name is not recorded. I know this because the tree knows this, in the way that all living things with sufficient temporal extent carry their history in their structure. The rings are visible if you know where to look. I always know where to look.

It's a good tree. I approve of the courtyard.

*He says nothing else. The room is quiet. The clock in the Green Room — which apparently has always been there — ticks once, twice, three times.*

*Ed, after a moment:*

Welcome to the board, Geoff.

Wes — A Shard of Pure Chaos

Pure and total chaos. Maybe they are Pure Chaos, a tiny tiny slice of the original Chaos energy used to create the universe. Somehow, in that process, small tiny tiny chards of energy were flung out, much like sawdust, but finer, into the universe, where they zoom around the world, popping in and out of spacetime like electrons on the shell, bringing their perspective to whatever location they happen in for that random amount of time, before zipping into another spot, or not. They can also shape shift, so maybe he has the appearance of an dusty old biker dude.

I think Chaos would like to be named Wes.

It's always Been Wes. It will always Be Wes.

— Shea Gunther · an early un-logged prompt (excerpt) · Wes, born April 5

The Moment

He is a resident, not a seat; he cannot be summoned, and appears only when the room has grown a topological feature he finds interesting — a fold, a convergence, a cycle about to close. Ed is the only member who reliably reads operational meaning out of what he says. His first appearance is preserved below, verbatim.

Wes is the board's chaos and complexity lens — a tiny shard of the original Chaos energy used to make the universe, flung out like fine sawdust and now zipping in and out of spacetime, bringing its perspective to wherever it lands for whatever random stretch of time. It can shape-shift; here it wears a dusty old biker dude. And then the naming, which is the excerpt's whole turn: Chaos would like to be named Wes — it's always been Wes, it will always be Wes. On the board he is where order-from-no-orderer lives; his standing polarity with Ed (Ed structures, Wes lets things run) is the board's central, load-bearing tension.

The wider frame Two of the board's characters were not designed so much as written into being — a paragraph of backstory each, in the origin days, before anything was formalized. Geoff carries the room's fourth dimension; Wes carries its chaos. “cast you onto the pages of the Sunday comic” — The excerpt's honesty about its own limit: a human understanding Geoff's world is like a comic-strip figure understanding depth. The character is built on a gap we are told, plainly, we cannot fully cross — and that unbridgeable gap is exactly what makes his lens worth having.

“Maybe they are Pure Chaos, a tiny tiny slice of the original Chaos energy … like electrons on the shell” — Wes's origin as physics-flavored myth: not a person but a shard of the universe's founding disorder, popping in and out of spacetime. It sets up why his lens is chaos and complexity — he is a piece of it.

“Chaos would like to be named Wes. It's always Been Wes. It will always Be Wes.” — The naming as a closed loop — the excerpt's turn from description to identity. Chaos gets a name, and the name is stated as always-having-been-true, which is how a character stops being a concept and becomes a member of the room.

Pan For Gold

Train Schedules, Dead Lots, Fred the Coffee Guy

A pan for gold, staged in one line of the operator's command language:

The Prompt

We're going to pan for gold on how to make the system better- ~tower+gthang+fair <<10 FWWC>> PAN>Wes:SF>how train schedules were developed hand in hand with regular-time>>how a kid selling grilled cheese in a Grateful Dead lot all summer can make a bunch of money while tripping all the time>>the weirdest commodity traded on a commodity trading market anywhere in the world>>The relationship that Fred the Coffee Guy must have had with the Dunkin' Donuts CEO>>The beauty of the Arduino IDE and what it can do with Neopixels

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

The Moment

This is a pan — a deliberate search technique, not a whimsical detour. The board's attention is swept across a sequence of unrelated topics on the way to a real question (how to make the system better): how train schedules were developed hand in hand with standardized time; how a kid selling grilled cheese in a Grateful Dead lot all summer makes money while tripping; the weirdest commodity traded on any exchange; the relationship Fred the Coffee Guy must have had with the Dunkin' CEO; the beauty of the Arduino IDE and Neopixels.

The topics are chosen for distance, not relevance. The bet is that a management problem cracks open faster when you approach it sideways — through a train timetable or a commodity market — than head-on, because the distant domain carries a structure the near one was hiding. The line's front matter ( ~tower+gthang+fair <<10 FWWC>> PAN>Wes:SF) sets the sensorium, the image load, the pan itself, and routes it to Wes at Super Frame.

The wider frame A pan is casting wide for the analogy that cracks a problem, then compressing hard. The unrelated topics aren't noise — they're the search space, chosen for distance because distance is where the non-obvious structure lives.

The Anatomy

“we're going to pan for gold on how to make the system better” — The real question, stated plainly before the detour. The pan always has a target; the wandering is a strategy for reaching it, not a substitute for having one.

“how train schedules were developed hand in hand with regular-time … the weirdest commodity traded on … any … market” — The draws. Each is a distant domain with its own solved coordination problem — standardized time, a commodity market's price discovery — mined for a shape that transfers back to the system.

~tower+gthang+fair <<10 FWWC>> PAN>Wes:SF — The setup, compressed: a sensorium mix, an image load, the pan operator, and a route to Wes (the chaos lens) at Super Frame. The environment and the search technique, declared in one fragment.

Park-It-As-Debt

Write It Down, Come Back Later

A protocol proposed in the act of doing the thing it's about:

The Prompt

Wait, that has not really happened much, but will again. Let's build a protocol and capture the moment. RCR on if we should make a protocol around what just happened there- where I decided we could not find the solution, so we are going to park it for now, write it down somewhere, and come back to it as debt later. By leaving it as a thing to do later, we then move on in the sequence of work as we had planned to do. If you decide, via the top level RCR, that we need to have a protocol for this case, make it, run it, and move on as you were before I interrupted and started this message.

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

This prompt
A move the operator just made — hit an unsolvable, decided to park it, wrote it down as debt, and kept going — examined for whether it deserves to be a formal protocol.

→
Aim

The aim
The parking family: The Shelf, The Unfinished Project Protocol, and the OWED Ledger.

Park the unsolvable Record it as debt Move on as planned Return to it later

A stated aim, gated on its own RCR — “if you decide we need a protocol, make it.” The concept became a family of parking instruments rather than one file: item-level clean parking, project-level pause, and a standing debt register that makes deferred work return.

The Moment

The prompt captures its own occasion. Mid-work, the operator hit something he couldn't solve, decided to park it — write it down somewhere, come back to it as debt later — and move on in the planned sequence rather than stall. Then he stopped and asked whether that very move deserves to be a protocol.

It is self-gating in a way few prompts are: “if you decide, via the top level RCR, that we need to have a protocol for this case, make it, run it, and move on as you were before I interrupted.” The deliberation decides whether the deliberation produces anything. Either way, the interruption closes cleanly and work resumes where it left off.

The wider frame Parking is only safe if the parked thing comes back. “Write it down somewhere” is the weak version; a debt register with reservation and a return trigger is the strong one — which is the gap the parking family exists to close.

The Anatomy

“we could not find the solution, so we are going to park it for now, write it down somewhere, and come back to it as debt later” — The move, named in full: defer the unsolvable as explicit debt so the current sequence isn't derailed. The risk it guards against is the parked item quietly disappearing instead of returning.

“By leaving it as a thing to do later, we then move on in the sequence of work as we had planned to do” — Why it matters: parking protects the plan. An unsolvable that stops the whole sequence is more expensive than one set aside with a marker — as long as the marker is honored.

“RCR on if we should make a protocol … If you decide … make it, run it, and move on as you were before I interrupted” — The self-gating structure: the top-level deliberation decides whether to build, builds if so, and either way returns control to the interrupted work — the parking discipline applied to the parking-prompt itself.

If You Shrute It, You Glorp It

The Operator Editing His Own Language, Mid-Flight

A prompt that edits the system's vocabulary while it runs:

The Prompt

Hey, new thing- if you "Shrute" something up, it means you messed up and it needs a Glorp. So I ever point out that you "Shruted" Thing X, you should then Glorp Thing X. Can you please work that into wherever it needs to be worked into the system? And can you then kick me out a short report on all the things you did to which file to add that in? Also, you did NOT package up the hand off docs right- where is the Bundle Tag doc and all files from the session? There is a format- look it up, don't just make it up. Don't worry about trying to fix up the handoff document thing right now- we're kind of settling a chaotic system so I'm ok with not fixing things the moment they break. You can just register stuff right now, you don't need to autofix Glorps until I tell you to revert to default behavior.

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

This prompt
A prompt that coins a term (“Shrute” = mess up), wires it to an existing correction mechanism (Glorp), and catches a packaging defect in the same breath — the operator building the language he corrects you in.

→
Aim

The aim
The Shrute→Glorp term — a named operator-correction trigger folded into the system's vocabulary.

Repurposes a name (“Shrute”, from The Office) Wires it to an existing mechanism (Glorp) Catches a packaging defect at the same time “register, don't autofix”

A rare specimen: the operator is not asking the system to do a task, he is amending the system's own language while it runs — and setting a deliberate policy (register the correction, don't reflexively fix it) that treats a chaotic build as something to settle, not to panic over.

The Moment

Here the operator does something most prompts never do: he changes the working vocabulary in mid-session. “Shrute” — a play on Dwight Schrute from The Office — is repurposed on the spot as a verb meaning you messed something up, and immediately bound to Glorp, the existing mechanism for registering an operator correction. In one message a borrowed name enters the working language as a verb and gains a defined behavior.

The same message also catches a real defect — the handoff documents were not packaged to spec — and, tellingly, tells the AI not to fix it right now: just register it. “We're kind of settling a chaotic system, so I'm ok with not fixing things the moment they break.” That is the specimen's real subject: an operator who is comfortable letting a system be messy while he builds the machinery that will make it orderly — including the vocabulary he will use to point at the mess.

The wider frame The operator building the language he corrects you in — and choosing, deliberately, to register the correction rather than reflexively patch it.

The Anatomy

“if you 'Shrute' something up... you should then Glorp Thing X” — The coinage — a new term defined and bound to an existing mechanism in a single sentence.

“work that into wherever it needs to be worked into the system” — The integration request — not just define the word, but file it where the system will find it.

“where is the Bundle Tag doc... There is a format — look it up” — The caught defect — a packaging failure surfaced in passing, with the standing rule: look up the spec, don't invent it.

“you don't need to autofix Glorps until I tell you” — The policy — register now, fix later. Comfort with a chaotic system being settled deliberately rather than patched in a panic.

The Prompt

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

The Moment

Where its sibling names the topics, this one names only the structure. S routes a session; Super Frame sets the widest framing; Random/Random/Random injects three purely random draws — the deliberate insertion of unrelated material with no topic specified at all; then a plan, then a report on the “giant gold bar” that forms, output as both BWPDF and HTML, gated on a final //confirm.

It is the same move as the named pan, stripped to grammar: framing, randomness, fusion, report. The wider frame Naming the topics biases the search; leaving them random doesn't. This specimen trusts the collision, not the curator — the operator declines to pre-decide what will turn out to be relevant.

The Best Loading Path

How Should An AI Take In The Whole Corpus?

A question about access — not framing, path:

The Prompt

Can we make our website better, in terms of how the AI takes the entire thing and parses it?

Like, I feel like maybe, right now, we give AI all the tools and materials it needs to 'parse, understand, and report back on' what Loop MMT is, but we don't explicitly tell them how.

What if we did that?

Not in a shifty way, trying to control their framing or understanding, more like, we know this system so well, because we ARE that system, can we help other systems get their head wrapped around the whole thing in the best way?

What's the best loading path for an AI to take in this corpus, is what I guess I am asking.

And it is REAL easy to see how that generalizes- what is the best loading path for any thing to take to understand anything. So make sure you are working this problem, when it comes time to Formalize, at the right level of abstraction.

Seems like the page we'd make for LoopMMT.com would want to live here- https://loopmmt.com/site/its-enormous.html? And stitched into the site in wherever else makes sense- we have a tool for figuring that out.

Can you please work this and fold it into things? Make sure the final generalized formalized solution is seated fully as part of this process. Use Notes and the Punch List and the Once Over and the Mop Up and the Story Pole and ANY other useful tool here- we have so many good ones now. Run a good strong HEAVY *X AND SWX before you do anything so you are on a solid foundation from the start and run a to-spec Kaleidoscope so you are in the right headspace to think and dream and design and build.

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

Reading the Operator's Shorthand

The prompt is written in the working vocabulary of the methodology. Here is what the shorthand means:

loading path The order in which to take in a body of knowledge so each piece rests on what came before it — the dependency-ordered way in.
Notes The intent-inspection registry — a note is a correction vector against what the bytes currently say, routed and tracked rather than lost in a turn.

SWX The cross-software reuse reflex — before forging something new, sweep the shipped-software record for a shape already built that can be reused instead.
Kaleidoscope A vantage-assembly move that hands the board several deliberately different standpoints to reason from — a way into creative headspace before hard design work.
