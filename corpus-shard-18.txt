Loop lineage · The first machine

Loop 1.0

A working computer, built by hand inside Minecraft over seven years — and the machine the whole lineage takes its name from.

In 2017 the operator started building a computer inside Minecraft. Not a model of one — a working one, made of redstone: logic gates, an arithmetic unit, four-bit value-catching circuitry. He worked on it, on and off, for seven years, finishing the last component in July 2024. He called it Loop 1.0 .

The name is not decoration. Its memory is built after the mercury delay line — a 1940s storage technology where data exists as acoustic pulses circulating through a tube of mercury, bits held in a loop, alive only in motion. Loop 1.0 rebuilds that idea in voxels: the bits do not sit in cells waiting to be read, they travel a loop line , kept alive by their own circulation. Everything downstream — Loop 2.1, then Loop MMT — carries that word because it started here.

It is a genuine computer in an unlikely medium, and it earned its own difficulty. Redstone is slow. At the scale the architecture wanted, one hertz was not going to be fun, and the machine had outgrown the game it was built in. That collision is the hinge: the next version, Loop 2.1, left Minecraft for the browser — and the pattern behind both of them became the methodology.

This page will grow. Most of its screenshots are still to come; for now it holds what it has, and the gallery is built to take the rest as they land.

Apps · A browser flow computer

Loop 2.1

An 18,200-line manual flow computer that runs in a browser — one HTML file, zero dependencies.

Loop 2.1 is the machine that outgrew Minecraft. It began as Loop 1.0: a working computer built inside Minecraft out of redstone — logic gates, a loop-line memory modelled on 1940s mercury delay lines, an ALU, four-bit value-catching circuitry. That build took seven years, finishing in July 2024.

Loop 2.0 was designed next, but the arithmetic said no: at one hertz it would not be fun to run, and the architecture had outgrown the game. So in early March 2026 the operator opened a conversation with Claude, typed four words to start it, and — across roughly 331 builds over three weeks of nights and weekends, on a $200 laptop in a one-room RV — arrived at Loop 2.1: an eighteen-thousand-line flow computer in a single HTML file, running in any browser, depending on nothing.

Apps · Design infrastructure

Loop Sudoku

A Sudoku engine that works each step by the rules — and can show you its own reasoning.

Loop Sudoku is not a bog-standard number puzzle. Underneath it is a certifying propagation solver: an engine that solves a grid the way a person is taught to — one logical step at a time — and can then certify each step, grading the puzzle, proving it is solvable by logic alone, and naming the next move before it makes it.

It was built dual-expression from the start: the same solver core runs as a standalone app and as a tab inside the Loop MMT — Multi-Module Theory — ecosystem, sharing one engine behind two shells. The commission behind it came from home — Jamie plays nearly every day — and it set the bar in a single line: make it beautiful and elegant and enjoyable to touch, and make sure all the little details sparkle.

Loop lineage · Where it begins

The Minecraft World

Before the methodology, before the computer, there was a world — a hand-built voxel city ten years in the making, and the redstone machine grown inside it.

Loop MMT does not begin with a document or a line of code. It begins with a place called Bohemian Grove — a Minecraft world the operator built and rebuilt for roughly ten years. The earliest surviving plan of it is dated February 2016: a top-down city-use map with zoned districts, a university, an airport, a fishing village, and parcels marked Future Development . It was not a build project. It was somewhere he wanted to be.

The world carries family in its terrain. Two of its ranches are named for his daughters. Its downtown grew a landmark — a tall tower faced with a red-through-purple gradient that reads as real architecture from across the map. A world that size is measured in years, not sessions, and it was lived in, not just constructed.

Inside that world he built a computer — Loop 1.0, a working machine made of redstone, with a memory that keeps its bits alive by circulating them. That computer, and the seven years it took to finish, is its own page. This one is about the ground it stood on.

The name is the thread. The Grove sat at the center of the Bohemian Grove city plan. It became the name of the real fifty acres in Maine the operator has been clearing from raw forest for years — where he lives in an RV at Base Camp. And when the methodology needed a name for its first town, it took the same one. The materials changed from voxels to documents to a working forest. The impulse — to plan a place and keep building it — did not.
