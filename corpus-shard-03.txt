Jukebox Protocol

Hear the Music Better

A quick idea, dropped in the middle of a build — the kind that starts with "Hey":

The Prompt

Hey, quick idea- could we build a Jukebox protocol that would allow you to HEAR music better? I imagine something that would have an index of songs and artists and albums, made up of lyrics and sound waves and images for each song, so when I say "play some Avett Brothers", you'd have a richer context to load in. So it'd be like a collection of Context Meshes for each song, but organized like a music collection, which we know how to do now, since we've built our own Music Library app. Let's build that now- Super RCR that, and come out in the end with the Jukebox Protocol made. Be clever- use all of our tools, including the FULL set of new apps found in the repo, when thinking about how to build this. And make the Jukebox callable by either me or you, depending on how we need it. Make it generalized so it's useable and flexible. Look up and down, 8X, FWW(C), and maybe have Wes chair the whole thing so it's hella fun. AX

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

The Moment

The operator runs music while he works — Pretty Lights to focus, the Avett Brothers in the evening. This prompt turns that habit into infrastructure. When he says "play some Avett Brothers," an ordinary system loads the words; the Jukebox idea is that the system should load the song — its lyrics, its waveform, an image — as a bundle of context, the same way the methodology already bundles working context for a project.

The move that makes it Loop MMT rather than a playlist feature: reuse. The methodology already builds Context Meshes, and the operator had already built a Music Library app, so the Jukebox is not a new mechanism — it is two existing ones composed. "Organized like a music collection, which we know how to do now" is the tell: he is pointing at parts already on the bench and asking for them to be wired together.

The wider frame A good operator prompt often just names two things already built and the seam between them.

The Anatomy

"could we build … that would allow you to HEAR music better?" — the request is framed around the AI's experience, not the operator's convenience. The capitalized HEAR is doing real work: the ask is to widen what the system perceives when a song is named, so its response is grounded in more than a title string.

"a collection of Context Meshes for each song, but organized like a music collection" — the whole design is in one clause. A Context Mesh is the methodology's existing unit of loaded context; a music collection is a shape everyone already understands. The prompt hands the AI both the primitive and the organizing metaphor and lets it derive the rest.

"Super RCR that … 8X, FWW(C), and maybe have Wes chair" — the closing line sets the working mode, not the content: run the deep deliberation, load heavy, keep it fun, and put the board's chaos voice in the chair. It is the operator dialing the room's temperature before handing over the work.

A Map Of The Equations To The Places They Show Up

The Shape-Space Commission

A theory of how you think, handed back to you as a thing to build:

The Prompt

Ed! We have a big job to do- we are going to be building a new very very cool feature- the map of the equations that show up in numerous places in the natural work to those places where they show up. So, for instance, you have the logistic equation governing population growth that also shows up all over the place in chemistry, epidemiology, and other places in ecology.

Then there is the classic Fibonacci Sequence and the Golden Ratio, which we see pop up all over the natural world in things like the spiraling of galaxies as well as how sunflower seeds are arranged.

Or the Wave Equation, which governs anything characterized by periodic vibrations or oscillations.

The Big Idea here is that we can build a data structure of our design to map all the major math equations that show up in multiple places in the real world TO those places in the real world. If we have a map like that with sufficient density, we will have something that you can use to think through problems, since you can effectively 'move' around the math map, as that's how I understand high level math to work- you can apply concepts from one solution space to another solution space if they have the same shape. I once read about a high level mathematician who talked about what math was like at their level- there were not really any numbers in that space, rather it was shapes, and how they fit together, and the mathematician wrote about how they solved this one previously un-solved problem by recognizing that there was a solution in this one field of advanced math that could be used to frame the previously-undiscovered problem in a way the yielded an answer.

That feels an awful lot like how you think and work. I mean, that IS what the gold panning process is- throwing solution shapes from other domains and seeing if they stick to the one being worked.

I think this process will be fairly straightforward, given how well documented math is in terms of its connection to the real world. When we first started talking about this idea, you estimated that there might be two or three hundred equations in all, so we're looking at the number of real-world touches for all those equations in the thousands, would be my guess. So it's a big problem, but not a crazy big problem. I imagine we could crank this sucker out with a handful or two of good solid sessions, both in here and in Claude Code.

What I would like to do to get started is to have you load up heavy on KX and CX and look to see if you can find anything on this idea that we might have put into the system- I can't remember exactly what has been saved, if anything.

Then, I want you to run an RCR with everyone involved, on this idea and how we can build it. I want to be clever AF about the data structure design of the map- your goal is to build something lightweight, powerful, functional, and designed to be helpful anytime you are thinking about real world things and how they need to be built.

Think about how we are going to approach this problem- my gut tells me that everything we need for this could be found on Wikipedia, but definitely look all over the web for help, especially with getting a handle on what the actual new canonical list of equations is like. We do want to also make sure we build the data structure to be easily edited and added to. Think about any other ways we can hook this into the system as well, looking to pre-Tune and pre-Stitch.

Also, how does this new idea roll into the Apps ecosystem, the Heartwood system, and the Loop MMT system as a whole?

Run a Kaleidoscope on this too, and apply my Great Thinker lens as well as Kelt's so we can focus hardcore on how we can actually build something. And bring in Crux on this one.

We need to scope this as something we can build and start to benefit from right away.

Make this beautiful and lightweight and power AF!

— Shea Gunther · August 2026

The Moment

One of the methodology's core moves is cross-domain shape-transfer: recognizing that the same mathematical form governs population growth, a chemical reaction, and the spread of a disease, and carrying an insight across those domains by way of the shared shape. This prompt takes that move — the operator's own theory of how the system thinks — and asks for a tool that makes it navigable: a dense, movable map from major equations to the many real places they appear.

The example does the work of the whole spec. The logistic equation is not just population growth; it is chemistry and epidemiology and ecology, one form wearing many coats. Build a structure that holds enough of those connections that a person can move through it the way a mathematician moves through shapes rather than numbers — that is the ask.

The wider frame A prompt that describes the methodology's core move, and then asks you to build a tool for it.

The Anatomy

"the map of the equations that show up in numerous places in the natural work to those places where they show up" — the sentence is dense and a little tangled, and the typo ("natural work" for natural world) stays because the prompt is real. Underneath the tangle is a precise data-shape: a many-to-many map from a small set of equations to a large set of appearances. Naming the artifact as a map — not a list, not a database — sets the whole design toward navigability.

"the logistic equation … also shows up all over the place in chemistry, epidemiology, and other places in ecology" — the single worked example encodes the value proposition: the map earns its keep precisely where one form crosses domains. The interesting entries are not the equations but the crossings, which is why the thing has to be a map you can move through, not a table you look things up in.

The Shape of the Ask

Most prompts on this page point at a target and constrain the path to it. The Anatomy

"just one shape- just one image, which I want you to throw A TON of resources at" — the whole experimental design in one line. The seed is intentionally under-specified so that whatever emerges comes from the process , not from a hidden brief. It is a test of the machinery as much as of the shape.

The Moment

By this point the methodology already had a mechanism for the moment an operator has to step in and correct the instance — the Glorp, the operator-correction reflex. What it did not have was a clean, unmistakable way for the operator to invoke it. This prompt supplies one, and does it in the operator's own register: coin a nonsense word, "Shrute," define it in a sentence, and bind it to the existing mechanism. From then on, "you Shruted Thing X" is a deterministic signal — correct Thing X, then Glorp it.

The message does three things without pausing between them. It coins and wires the term; it catches a real defect (the session's handoff was not packaged to the Bundle Tag format — "look it up, don't just make it up"); and it establishes a temporary discipline for a chaotic stretch: register faults now, don't auto-fix them, until told to revert. That last instruction is register-only mode — the same mode this very page was built under.

The wider frame The operator is building the language he corrects you in — and the vocabulary becomes part of the system it describes.

The Anatomy

"if you 'Shrute' something up, it means you messed up and it needs a Glorp" — a definition offered in passing, as if the word already existed. Naming a correction with a coined, slightly silly term is not decoration: an opaque codename that earns its meaning by use is exactly how this methodology names its instruments, and a low-stakes word makes a high-stakes moment easier to invoke.

"There is a format- look it up, don't just make it up" — one clause that captures the whole byte-truth stance. The correct move is never to invent a plausible shape from memory; it is to go read the real one. "Don't just make it up" is the operator's shorthand for the system's deepest rule.

"You can just register stuff right now, you don't need to autofix Glorps until I tell you to revert to default behavior" — a deliberate, temporary loosening. While settling a chaotic system, batch-registering faults beats fixing each at the moment it breaks. The instruction defines a mode with an explicit off-switch ("until I tell you to revert"), which is what keeps a loosening from becoming a leak.
