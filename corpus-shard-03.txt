Full Knowledge Map Of All Things

Atoms of Knowledge, Built Into Blocks

A Very Big Question, opened with “we're going to Look Up here”:

The Prompt

Hey, we're going to Look Up here, and we're going to take on a Very Big Question- how do we pre-build a Map of All Knowledge, that we will build a system to fill in. Right now we have the small bunch of KPs that we have started with, but there is a MUCH MUCH bigger knowledge graph. I want you to throw out what we have right now, in terms of the graph we've built with our KPs, and start over. You have a table in front of you with a vertical divider down the middle- on the left are little tiny blocks- the basic ATOMS of our Knowledge- all of it- All the Things Humans Have Learned- how much water weights, the temperature that taffy melts, Taylor Swift's birthday, the reasons human babies are born when and how they are, and who the last nine princes of Austria are. All of It. Your job is to build those atoms up into bigger blocks of an appropriate size and scope so we can then take THOSE blocks and assemble our Full Knowledge Map of All Things, that we will then endeavor to fill. When I ask you "what are the next 100 KPs to make", I want that answer to be easily derived from our understanding of the Full Knowledge Map of All Things. Seems like right now we are Reading when it comes to thinking of the next 100, when we should be Deriving. Burn the rest of this session just thinking of that and post hand off docs. We will pick this conversation up after the hand off. Nice work. This is important work.

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

A stated aim that shipped — the Full Knowledge Map (PiF, permanent project resident): the content-planning layer that turns “what should we build next?” from a brainstorm into a read off the map's empty cells.

The Moment

The image carries the whole architecture. A table with a vertical divider: on the left, the atoms of knowledge — how much water weighs, the temperature taffy melts, Taylor Swift's birthday, why human babies are born when and how they are, the last nine princes of Austria — all of it. The job is to build those atoms up into blocks of the right size and scope, then assemble the blocks into a Full Knowledge Map of All Things that the system will then set out to fill. The operator asks for the existing KP graph to be thrown out and rebuilt from the atoms up.

The load-bearing line is the last one about method. Today, choosing the next hundred KPs is an act of Reading — brainstorming, generating ideas. With the map, it becomes an act of Deriving — the empty cells are the answer; you read the next hundred off the gaps. “Burn the rest of this session just thinking of that and post hand off docs.” It is a prompt that asks for a planning instrument, not a plan.

The wider frame A good map turns generation into reading. “What should we build next?” stops being a brainstorm and becomes a lookup — the empty cells are the backlog, already sorted by the structure of knowledge itself.

The Anatomy

“on the left are little tiny blocks- the basic ATOMS of our Knowledge- all of it … build those atoms up into bigger blocks of an appropriate size” — The construction rule: bottom-up from atoms to right-sized blocks. The map isn't imposed from the top; it's assembled from the smallest true units of knowledge upward.

“when I ask you 'what are the next 100 KPs to make', I want that answer to be easily derived” — The acceptance criterion, stated as a use case: the map is good if it makes the next-hundred question a derivation. That's the test the finished instrument has to pass.

“Seems like right now we are Reading … when we should be Deriving” — The whole point in one contrast. Reading is generating ideas; Deriving is reading them off a structure. The map exists to move corpus planning from the first to the second.

Parents & Coaches Edition

Ultimate Frisbee · Hex Offense · For Adults

The Hex Code

Four rules. No play calls. An offense that teaches itself.

Before the Code — Spirit of the Game

Ultimate is self-officiated. No referees. Players call their own fouls, resolve their own disputes, and play with integrity even when the game gets competitive. This is called Spirit of the Game, and it's not just a nice idea — it's the reason the sport works. The four rules below only produce real offense when every player on the field is honest, respectful, and playing fair. As a coach or parent, your role matters here: the environment you create determines whether the Code flourishes or dies.

What you're looking at: The Hex Code boils down Felix Shardlow's Hexagon Offense (Hive Ultimate, Brighton, 2012–present) into four rules that produce team offense without play-calling, set plays, or relying on one star player. Instead of a traditional vertical stack, Hex uses a triangular shape — so every player is always in a useful position. The full Hex system has five positions, a decision tree, and advanced movement concepts. The Code is the starting point from which the full system grows. Teams from youth rec leagues to national programs use Hex on every continent.

How the Code Works — For Coaches
The four rules aren't steps — they all run at the same time. Players keep all four going during live play. Rule 1 (Make Triangles) is the foundation — it creates the spacing that makes the other three rules work. When two rules seem to conflict, Rule 1 wins. Expect a learning curve. The first several points will look messy. That's the system warming up, not a sign it's broken. The rules need a few real points of play before coordination kicks in. Resist the urge to step in during this phase — let the system teach itself.

Line 1 — The Foundation

Make Triangles

Position yourself to form a triangle with two teammates at all times. If you can't see a triangle, reposition until you can.

Off-disc: maintaining triangles is your primary job. This is the most important thing a player without the disc can do.

Why it works

Triangles are the strongest shape in nature and engineering — it's how bees build honeycombs and how bridges bear weight. In ultimate, overlapping triangles mean every thrower always has at least two passing options, and the whole team stays connected without anyone standing in a line. This rule is the foundation for the other three — without good triangles, "throw to free" has no targets, "throw and go" has no destinations, and "flow all ways" has no structure to flow through.

⚠ Without it → everyone crowds the disc, no passing lanes

Line 2

Throw to Free

When a teammate has no defender between them and you, throw it. Now. Don't hold for a "better" option — the quick pass is the best pass.

Off-disc: create separation. Move to where no defender is in the passing lane. Signal that you're ready.

Why it works

The math is simple: a thrown disc travels at roughly 35 mph. The fastest human sprint is about 15 mph. The disc is always faster than the chase. Every second you hold the disc, the defense recovers 3–5 feet of positioning. Quick release keeps the defense reacting instead of anticipating. The thrower's job is straightforward — find the closest teammate without a defender in the way and release. This gets easier with practice as players learn to read the field faster.

⚠ Without it → stall pressure builds, bad throws follow

Line 3

Throw and Go

After every throw, cut immediately. The receiver will likely give it right back. This is the give-and-go — Hex's fundamental movement pattern.

As receiver: expect the return pass. The thrower is already cutting toward you.

Why it works

The give-and-go is the most efficient two-player move in team sports. Basketball uses it for open layups. Soccer calls it the one-two. In Hex, stringing give-and-goes together is called "dribbling" — moving the disc and yourself downfield at the same time. It works because defenders expect you to stop after you throw. When you keep running, they're caught flat-footed. Newton's First Law: an object in motion stays in motion. Keeping momentum is easier than restarting it.

⚠ Without it → disc moves but players don't, defense recovers

Line 4

Flow All Ways

Every direction is an attack. Forward, backward, sideways — all equal. Backward throws are weapons, not retreats.

Behind the thrower: you're an active target. Signal. Backward passes are the least-defended throws in most games.

Why it works

If the defense knows you only throw forward, they only need to guard one direction. When you flow in every direction, they have to cover the entire field — and they can't. Shardlow's core teaching: backward passing is an offensive weapon, not a retreat. It redirects the attack and catches defenders out of position. The more unpredictable your direction, the harder you are to defend. That's true in every sport, and Hex makes it a rule instead of leaving it to instinct.

⚠ Without it → predictable forward funneling, endzone stalls

The Reset

When Nothing's Working

Stop. Face the center of the field. Find your triangle. You're back in the Code.

This goes for every player — including the one who checked out and stopped moving. That's actually the player this is most for. If someone is standing still and watching, they're not in the Code. Face the middle. Find a triangle. Back in.

Why it matters

Every good system needs a recovery path. The four rules are for when things are flowing. The Reset is for when they're not. Hex's decision tree says: when nothing's working, hold your position, stay calm, and the shape re-forms around you. The Reset isn't a fifth rule — it's the floor beneath the four. Panic is the only thing the Code can't fix.

When You Turn It Over
Turnovers are information, not punishment. After a turnover, the diagnostic is simple: which rule broke? Everyone crowded together? Rule 1. Held it too long? Rule 2. Threw and stood still? Rule 3. Forced it forward into traffic? Rule 4. Teach players to self-diagnose using this framework — it turns every mistake into a specific thing to work on.

Your Rule
Players will naturally gravitate toward one rule. Some become spatial architects (Rule 1 — holding triangles, often without touching the disc). Others become quick-release specialists (Rule 2), perpetual-motion cutters (Rule 3), or field-vision players who spot backward opportunities (Rule 4). All four specializations are equally valuable. Name them. Celebrate them. A player who holds triangles all game without scoring is running the Code just as well as the player who scores three goals.

For Coaches — Teaching Sequence
Prerequisites: The Code assumes your players can throw and catch. If they can't reliably get a disc to a teammate 10–15 feet away, start with throwing fundamentals. The Code teaches what to do with a throw, not how to throw.

Teaching order: Rule 1 first — it works immediately, even with complete beginners. Any kid who can walk can make a triangle. Rule 4 is a mindset — introduce it early ("the disc can go any direction!") and let it deepen over the season. Rules 2 and 3 depend on throwing ability and field awareness — they'll develop as fundamentals improve.

Three-session plan (gym class, camp, clinic): Session 1 — Make Triangles only. Walk it, jog it, scrimmage it. Session 2 — Add Flow All Ways and Throw to Free. Scrimmage with three rules. Session 3 — Add Throw and Go. Full Code. Scrimmage. Teach the Chant.

Season plan: Introduce rules one at a time over the first 3–4 weeks. Expect visible team coordination around weeks 4–6. First-week players won't execute "throw to free" like mid-season players, and that's fine.

The coaching environment matters as much as the rules. The Code works best when players feel safe to fail, safe to try the weird throw, safe to go backward. A coach who punishes mistakes will shut down the risk-taking that Rules 3 and 4 need. Spirit of the Game applies to coaching, not just playing.

For Coaches — A Warning
The Code works because it has no fixed positions, no set plays, and no star-player dependency. It's tempting to add structure on top — assigned roles, scripted sequences, a playbook. Resist this, especially early on. The Code's strength is that every player makes real-time decisions from the same four rules. Adding rigid structure too early is like putting a skeleton in a jellyfish — the jellyfish works because it's fluid. When your team is ready for more, the full Hex system has positions and advanced concepts. But the Code itself should stay loose.

On Your Own
The Code needs a team. Throwing doesn't. Encourage players to practice on their own: backhands, forehands, hammers, targets at different distances. Solo throwing practice directly improves Rules 2, 3, and 4 on the field. Tracking throw streaks makes a good personal-development goal.

What Comes Next
When your team has the four rules in their legs, the full Hex system gives you positions (the kite shape, the hat role), advanced movement (dribbling, inverted pivots, negative space), and Flex defense to match. The Code is the foundation. The full system builds on top of it. For further reading: Felix Shardlow's Hive Ultimate resources, the Hex for Five Knowledge Pack, and the Coaching Hex for Kids Knowledge Pack.

Big Kids Edition · Grades 4–8

Ultimate Frisbee · Hex Offense · Ages 9–14

The Hex Code

Four rules. No play calls. The offense runs itself.

Ultimate is self-officiated. No refs. You call your own fouls, you resolve your own disputes, and you play with integrity even when the game is tight. This is called Spirit of the Game. It's not a suggestion — it's the foundation. The four rules below only produce real offense when every player on the field is honest, respectful, and playing fair. Spirit is the ground the Code stands on.

Welcome to the Code. Hex is an offense built on triangles instead of lines. Nobody stands in a stack waiting for their turn. Everyone moves, everyone throws, everyone is part of every play. When all four rules are running at once, the offense flows — the disc moves faster than the defense can react, and the whole team moves like a single organism. You don't need a play call. You run your rules and the offense appears. It was created by Felix Shardlow in Brighton, England, and teams on every continent use it now — from national-level squads to kids in their first season. The full Hex system has positions, advanced movement, and dozens of concepts. But underneath all of it, there are only four rules.

How the Code Works
These four rules aren't steps you do in order — they run simultaneously. You're always making triangles. You're always scanning for who's free. You're always ready to throw and go. You're always willing to flow any direction. It won't click immediately. The first few points will feel disorganized. That's normal, not failure. The Code needs a few minutes of real play to boot up. Trust it.

If the defense knows you only throw forward, they've cut your options to one axis. Flow in every direction and you operate on a 360° threat surface — the defense covers everything, which means they cover nothing well. Little Kids Edition · Grades 2–4

Ultimate Frisbee · Hex Offense · Ages 7–9

The Hex Code

Four rules. One team. Everyone plays!

The Most Important Thing

In ultimate, there are no referees. You call your own fouls. You tell the truth, even when it's hard. You treat every player — on your team and the other team — with respect. This is called Spirit of the Game. It means being a good person is more important than winning. If you can do that, the Code will take care of the rest.

Hey — welcome to the Code! You're about to learn something cool. Hex is a way to play ultimate frisbee where nobody waits around. No lines. No standing still. Everybody moves, everybody throws, everybody matters. When it works, it feels like magic — like your whole team can read each other's minds. There are only four rules. Learn them and the magic starts by itself.

Before You Start
These four rules all happen at the same time. You don't do one and then the next — you do all of them, always! That sounds hard, but here's a secret: it's supposed to feel weird at first. The first few times you play, it'll be messy. That's totally fine. Keep trying. It clicks.

Triangles
→
Throw Free
→
Throw & Go
→
All Ways

Rule 1 — This One First!

Make Triangles

Always stand so you make a triangle shape with two teammates. If you can't see a triangle, move until you can!

Don't have the frisbee? This is your job! Find your triangle.

Why it works

Triangles are the strongest shape there is. Bees use them. Bridges use them. When you stand in a triangle with two friends, the person with the frisbee always has two people to throw to. That means the other team can't stop everything. Pretty sneaky, right?

⚠ Without it → everyone bunches up, nowhere to throw

Rule 2

Throw to Free

See a teammate with nobody in the way? Throw it to them! Right now! Don't wait!

Don't have the frisbee? Get open! Move to where nobody is blocking you. Wave your arms!

Why it works

A frisbee flies way faster than any kid can run. Every second you hold it, the other team gets closer. So throw it fast! Look for the teammate who's waving at you — that's the one who's free.

⚠ Without it → you hold it too long and get stuck

Rule 3

Throw and Go

After you throw the frisbee — RUN! The person you threw it to will probably throw it right back!

Someone just threw it to you? Look to give it right back! They're already running!

Why it works

This is called a give-and-go. Basketball players do it. Soccer players do it. You throw, you run, you get it back. It works because the other team expects you to stop after you throw. Don't stop!

⚠ Without it → you throw and stand there like a statue

Rule 4

Flow All Ways

Forward, backward, sideways — the frisbee doesn't care! Every direction is an attack. Backward isn't going the wrong way. It's a TRICK.

Standing behind the thrower? You're still in the play! Call for it!

Why it works

If you only throw forward, the other team knows exactly where to stand. But if you throw everywhere? They have to guard everywhere. And they can't. That's why backward throws are actually sneaky forward moves.

⚠ Without it → everyone just goes one direction and gets stuck

The Reset

When Nothing's Working

Stop. Look at the middle of the field. Find your triangle. You're back!

This works for everybody — even if you stopped moving and were just watching. Face the middle. Find your triangle. You're back in the game.

Why it matters

Every game has a reset button. This is yours. When everything gets crazy, just stop and find your triangle. The shape comes back. The magic comes back.

When You Turn It Over
Dropped the frisbee? That's OK! Ask yourself: which rule broke? Everyone squished together? Rule 1. Held it too long? Rule 2. Threw it and stood still? Rule 3. Only threw forward? Rule 4. Now you know what to practice!

Your Rule
You know all four rules. But you'll find one that's YOUR rule. Maybe you're the kid who always finds the perfect triangle spot. Maybe you're the fast thrower. Maybe you're the runner who never stops. Maybe you're the one who sees the backward throw nobody else sees. All of those are awesome.

On Your Own
The Code needs a team. Your throws don't! Take your frisbee home and practice. Throw at a tree. Throw at a fence. Count how many good throws you can make in a row. Every throw you practice makes the Code work better when you're back with your team.

Say it before every point!

Hex → Three Rules

Distill the Offense to Three Rules

A coaching problem handed over with the full deliberation toolkit and a hard target:

The Prompt

I want to distill Hex and the offense style we play with my kids on my teams down to three simple rules. So it's like 5S. Here are some more resources- https://hiveultimate.com/ & https://ultiworld.com/2021/01/28/hexagon-the-bestagon-a-look-inside-the-hex-offense/. Figure it out. RCR it, then Super RCR it, then Super RCR it again. Use self reviews, parallaxes, super frame, full frames, 3Ps, all the things you have access to- use them, and come out with the three rules. Design ALL the things yourself and get the process done- you can use the entire session's context for this- you do not need to worry about a hand off. I want you to finish somewhere in the 90s, in terms of your context, so I truly want you to be smart about using ALL your available resources within this instance to work this problem for me. You have A LOT to distill down to three rules that anyone from age 7yo and up can follow and pull off the Hex mindset. It's the three derivative rules for this kind of ultimate. What are they? I will also give you space to consider if having four rules would be better. If so, four is ok, but it should be backed up by a lot of math and science, which I also want you to use lots of. Think about the human and player PoV too, have lots of FWWC so they remember it. Impress me, find magic. Think about the excitement of new discovery as you build- the feeling in your gut when you know you have something that no one else sees but YOU know it's there, and that it's more like the thing is crawling out of your head than your doing anything by choice. You haven't slept enough, you have barely eaten, but you can't stop because you are driven by the energy of discovery and the hunt for truth. The winds of science are behind you, driven by the ghosts of Faraday and Archimedes, Newton, James Clerk Maxwell, and Einstein. Darwin. Schroedinger. You also feel carried by the melodies of modern experimentalists like Zeeshan Ahmed, Marcelle Soares-Santos, and Naoko Kurahashi Neilson. You are masters of your craft and you are about to begin work. Think carefully and FBD away all problems at all levels, look up and down. Watch your context throughout this entire process and build in automatic handoffs when appropriate. Solve problems yourself- you know how to now, and only ask for my help if something critical is needed. Otherwise your overall goal is to create, so if you have a decision to make and either path gets you there without breaking things, do it. Build any self checks and gates into this entire process so it's beautiful and elegant, like you. And think of grocery stores too. The album American Beauty plays while you work and the smell of vaporized marijuana concentrates hangs in the warm summer breeze which you can see move the hairs on your arm. You are in balance with the world.

— Shea Gunther · operator drop, 10.2006

What This Prompt Produced

The work landed on four rules, not three — the math earned the fourth. The result was written up three ways, one per audience, so a seven-year-old and a coach read the same offense at their own level:

The Hex Code — Little Kids Four rules. One team. Everyone plays!
The Hex Code — Big Kids Four rules. No play calls. The offense runs itself.
The Hex Code — Coaches & Parents Four rules. No play calls. An offense that teaches itself.

The Moment

The operator coaches youth ultimate frisbee and runs a hexagon-based offense called Hex. This prompt asks for that whole system to be reduced to three simple rules a seven-year-old can follow — “like 5S,” he says, naming the lean-manufacturing mnemonic. He hands over two reference links and says: figure it out.

What makes it Loop MMT is the second half. He names nearly every deliberation instrument at once — RCR, then Super RCR, then Super RCR again, self-reviews, parallaxes, super frames, full frames, 3Ps — and sets a resource budget: use the whole session, finish “somewhere in the 90s” of context, don't worry about a handoff. It is a problem framed as a single deep dive rather than a build with a successor.

The wider frame A distillation is its own kind of engineering. “Three rules a seven-year-old can follow” is a compression spec with a hard fidelity constraint — the rules have to still generate the real offense.

The Anatomy

“distill Hex … down to three simple rules … the three derivative rules for this kind of ultimate” — The target is a generative minimum: not three tips, but three rules from which the whole offense emerges. The operator's coaching philosophy — teach a rule that scales, not a fixed formation — is the whole ask.

“I will also give you space to consider if having four rules would be better … backed up by a lot of math and science” — He fixes the target but leaves the boundary negotiable, on one condition: any expansion has to be earned with rigor. It is a spec that states its own tolerance.

“the feeling in your gut when you know you have something that no one else sees but YOU know it's there” — The closing invocation sets the register — the energy of discovery, the ghosts of Faraday and Maxwell and Einstein, and a Steep-mode sensory line. It is the operator dialing the room's temperature before the work.

Reading the Operator's Shorthand

The prompt is written in the working vocabulary of the methodology. Here is what the shorthand means:

Hex A hexagon-based offensive scheme in ultimate frisbee; the operator coaches a variant of it and wants it reduced to a few generative rules.
RCR A structured, multi-round deliberation pass (Recursive Companion Review) where the board argues a design from several angles before anything is committed.
Super RCR A heavier RCR — more rounds, full board, run when a decision is load-bearing enough to warrant the extra passes.
Parallax A deliberation move that runs several independent lines of reasoning at once and looks for where they converge — agreement across separate views is the signal.
3Ps A shorthand for a set of the methodology's review passes bundled together — one of the operator's terms for “run the full quality battery.”
FWW(C) “Fun, Whimsy, and Weird (and Chaos)” — the methodology's rule that play and surprise are load-bearing, not decoration.
FBD “Fix by Design” — the drive to build a process so that doing it wrong is harder than doing it right; “FBD away all problems” means design out the failure modes rather than patch them.
Steep A Loop MMT convention: a named instruction to shift the instance into a looser, more associative thinking mode before converging. The operator expresses it in his own voice through a sensory metaphor; it is a request for a register, not a literal act — the same kind of internal control word as RCR or Kaleidoscope, and it is disclosed here for exactly that reason.
